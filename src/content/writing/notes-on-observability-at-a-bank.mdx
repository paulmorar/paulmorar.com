Observability at a payments business has the same shape as observability anywhere else — metrics, logs, traces, a dashboard nobody reads until it's too late — but the stakes shift the design. Here are a few things I wish someone had told me before I started building this layer.

## A trace is a story, not a debugger

The temptation, especially coming from a front-end background, is to treat traces as a kind of distributed `console.log`. They're not. A trace is the story of one unit of work, and the spans inside it are the chapters.

If your spans look like this:

```ts
span.setAttribute("step", "validating");
span.setAttribute("step", "calling-db");
span.setAttribute("step", "done");
```

…you've written a printf statement, not a trace. The trace abstraction wants:

```ts {3,7}
const span = tracer.startSpan("payments.transfer", {
  attributes: {
    "payment.amount": amount,
    "payment.currency": currency,
    "payment.corridor": `${from.country}-${to.country}`,
    "tenant.id": tenant.id,
  },
});
```

Highlight: those last two attributes are what let you answer "is this slow for _this_ customer?" six months from now, when the question matters.

## Cardinality is a budget, spend it deliberately

Every high-cardinality dimension you put on a metric multiplies your storage cost. `tenant_id` on every metric feels great until your bill triples. The rule we settled on:

- **Metrics**: low cardinality only. Tenant, region, status. Nothing user-shaped.
- **Logs**: medium cardinality. Request id, correlation id, tenant id.
- **Traces**: high cardinality is fine. That's what they're for.

Push expensive dimensions down the stack to the tool that's designed for them.

## Alerts should map to humans, not systems

The most expensive lesson, in pager hours, was this: an alert that doesn't tell a specific person what to do is just noise.

> If you can't write a one-sentence runbook for an alert, the alert isn't ready to ship.

We retired more than half of the alerts in our original setup using that rule. The remaining half got better names, owners, and runbook links. Pager load went down. Trust in the alerts went up.

## What I'd build first, if I were starting over

In order:

1. A consistent **correlation id** that flows from the public API edge through every internal hop. Without this, nothing else is worth doing.
2. **Structured logs** with that correlation id, before you touch metrics or traces.
3. A **single dashboard per service** that answers "is it healthy right now?" in five seconds. Owned by the team that runs the service.
4. **Traces**, with the cardinality budget above.
5. **Alerts**, last, after you've watched the dashboards for a while and know what "broken" actually looks like.

Most teams do this in reverse. Don't.
